In localized prostate cancer, does radical prostatectomy improve overall or prostate-cancer mortality compared with observation?

Absolute Effect

PIVOT randomized men with localized prostate cancer to radical prostatectomy or observation. Extended follow-up (median follow-up among survivors 18.6 years) found 246/346 men assigned to surgery (68%) and 269/367 assigned to observation (73%) died; HR for all-cause mortality 0.84 (95% CI 0.70–1.00; P=.044). The absolute risk reduction estimate was 5.7 percentage points with a 95% CI crossing zero, so the absolute magnitude remains uncertain.

Clinical interpretation

The patient-level value of an intervention depends on absolute event risk, time horizon, competing risks, toxicity and treatment burden—not on relative effect measures alone.

Limitations

Historical trials may use older staging, imaging, treatment techniques and supportive care. Subgroup findings are not automatically equivalent to randomized overall-trial evidence. This page summarizes the trial and does not replace the primary publication.

Updated long-term outcomes

In the extended follow-up analysis (median follow-up among survivors 18.6 years), 246/346 men assigned to surgery (68%) and 269/367 assigned to observation (73%) died. The hazard ratio for all-cause mortality was 0.84 (95% CI 0.70–1.00; P=.044); the estimated absolute risk reduction was 5.7 percentage points, but its 95% CI crossed zero (−0.89 to 12 percentage points). Restricted mean survival was 13.6 versus 12.6 years.

The results suggest a modest possible long-term advantage for surgery, with uncertainty around the absolute difference; they do not justify treating surgery as mandatory for every localized prostate cancer patient.

Current-practice context · reviewed 14 October 2026

PIVOT enrolled patients in an earlier diagnostic and treatment era. Modern management separates active surveillance for suitable low-risk disease from watchful waiting in patients with limited life expectancy, and individualizes definitive treatment for intermediate/high-risk disease. Use current risk classification, life expectancy, comorbidity, MRI/biopsy findings and patient preferences; do not translate PIVOT into a universal surgery-versus-no-treatment rule.

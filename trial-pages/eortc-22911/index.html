Prostate Cancer · Radiation · Post-prostatectomy

EORTC 22911

After radical prostatectomy in men with pathological high-risk features, does immediate postoperative prostate-bed radiotherapy improve biochemical control compared with observation and treatment at biochemical progression?

Phase III randomized trial N=1,005 Median follow-up 10.6 years

PopulationMen aged ≤75 years with previously untreated pT2–3, N0, M0 prostate cancer after radical prostatectomy and at least one adverse pathological feature: positive surgical margin, extracapsular/capsular extension or seminal-vesicle invasion. InterventionImmediate postoperative external-beam radiotherapy to the prostate bed: 60 Gy over 6 weeks. The trial represents historical conventional postoperative RT. ComparatorWait-and-see policy; radiotherapy or hormonal treatment was given if biochemical or clinical progression occurred. Randomization502 to postoperative RT; 503 to wait-and-see. Treatment was assigned centrally across 37 European institutions. Primary endpointBiochemical progression-free survival (BPFS), with biochemical progression defined by PSA rising above 0.2 µg/L in the long-term analysis. Trial registrationNCT00002511.

The pivotal trial predates contemporary IMRT, image guidance, dose escalation and modern postoperative target/OAR techniques, so its toxicity profile should not be assumed to represent current RT practice.

Important subgroup signal

Pathology review suggested that positive surgical margin status was the strongest predictor of greater biochemical benefit. In the pathology-reviewed subgroup, the hazard ratio for biochemical disease-free survival was 0.38 (95% CI 0.26–0.54) with positive margins versus 0.88 (95% CI 0.53–1.46) with negative margins; the interaction was significant (p<0.01). This was an exploratory subgroup analysis and should not be interpreted as a randomized comparison restricted to margin-positive patients.

What is the clinical takeaway?

EORTC 22911 established that immediate postoperative prostate-bed RT substantially improves biochemical control and locoregional control after prostatectomy in men with adverse pathological features.

However, the long-term trial did not demonstrate an overall-survival or distant-metastasis benefit, and the early clinical-progression benefit was no longer statistically significant at 10.6 years. RT increased late adverse effects.

The trial therefore answers the historical question of whether adjuvant RT can improve biochemical/local control. It does not by itself answer today's timing question of adjuvant RT versus very-early salvage RT, because the control strategy allowed treatment after biochemical progression and differs from modern PSA-triggered early salvage approaches.

Why this trial matters in the evidence pathway

EORTC 22911 → establishes the efficacy of postoperative RT for pathological high-risk disease → later trials test whether the same disease-control benefit can be achieved by reserving RT for early PSA relapse.

That distinction is central when interpreting EORTC 22911 alongside modern timing trials such as RADICALS-RT and GETUG-AFU 17.

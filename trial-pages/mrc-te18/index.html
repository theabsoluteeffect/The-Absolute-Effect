The Absolute Effect / Evidence / MRC TE18 / EORTC 30942
Germ Cell Tumours · Radiation · Stage I Seminoma

MRC TE18 / EORTC 30942

Clinical question

After orchidectomy for stage I seminoma, can adjuvant radiotherapy be safely reduced from 30 Gy in 15 fractions to 20 Gy in 10 fractions?

Trial context
Population
625 men with stage I testicular seminoma after orchidectomy, randomized 1995–1998.
Intervention
20 Gy in 10 fractions over 2 weeks.
Comparator
30 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks.
Primary design
Randomized non-inferiority dose-reduction trial.
Absolute Effect

Relapse control was essentially unchanged

At median follow-up of 61 months, there were 11 relapses with 20 Gy versus 10 with 30 Gy. The reported absolute difference in 2-year relapse rates was 0.7 percentage points. The hazard ratio for relapse was 1.11 (90% CI 0.54–2.28). The trial was powered to exclude an absolute increase in 2-year relapse of approximately 3–4 percentage points.

NNT/NNH: Not applicable. This was a non-inferiority dose-de-escalation comparison rather than a superiority trial.

Treatment burden / harm

Less acute morbidity with 20 Gy

Four weeks after starting treatment, moderate/severe lethargy was reported by 5% with 20 Gy versus 20% with 30 Gy. Inability to carry out normal work was reported by 28% versus 46%. By 12 weeks these differences had largely resolved.

Relative effect

Non-inferiority supported: HR for relapse 1.11 (90% CI 0.54–2.28). The upper confidence limit remained below the prespecified clinically important threshold for excess relapse used in the trial design.

Clinical interpretation

Reducing adjuvant seminoma radiotherapy from 30 Gy/15 fractions to 20 Gy/10 fractions substantially reduces short-term treatment burden without a clinically important increase in relapse at the studied follow-up. This is a dose-de-escalation result: the evidence supports using less treatment with similar disease control, rather than demonstrating that 20 Gy is superior to 30 Gy.

Limitations / maturity

The trial was designed around relapse rather than overall survival, which is difficult to improve in a disease with very high salvageability. Long-term risks such as second malignancy require longer follow-up than the primary report. The evidence applies to appropriately staged stage I seminoma treated after orchidectomy.
